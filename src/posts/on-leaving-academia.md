# On Leaving Academia

*Disclaimer:**This is not an article about how toxic academia has become, or how it can be improved.  There are plenty of other places you can go for that - maybe even on this blog at some point in the future. I wrote this essay for my own benefit - to clear the bile, and help me deal with a big transition in my life.*

*My experience quite specifically relates to astronomy - your mileage may vary.  If your immediate response is to comment “Well it wasn’t like that for me”, consider for a moment the benefits of listening to someone else’s story.*

In the late summer of 2012, I got together with some of my oldest friends to watch *The Dark Knight Rises* at the Glasgow Science Centre (our nearest IMAX at the time).

While it’s clearly not the best of the three Nolan Batman films, it has its moments.  One moment resonated quite deeply with me at the time:

![0bf9b9b0d27f82edd6bdc51f32ed431e](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/0bf9b9b0d27f82edd6bdc51f32ed431e.jpg)

I couldn’t help admiring the sentiment.  Giving your all for a cause you believed in - I felt like this was something I could aspire to.  At the time, I was two years into my first postdoc, limbering up for my first run at fellowship applications.  I was working on a paper that felt like “the next big thing”, which would prise open the market and be the first step towards making my position permanent (I still hadn’t learned that every good paper feels like that).

I felt that if I threw myself into my work, then it would happen - I would achieve the dream.  I had always been an overachiever, and failure was not part of the picture.

![maxresdefault](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/maxresdefault.jpg)

After seven failed cycles of fellowship applications, I rewatched The Dark Knight Rises.  I had moved institute, like academia demands, but I’d made the move as small as possible out of respect for my wife’s career, which was arguably doing better than mine.

I had published around 60 papers with over 1000 citations in total - most of them were first author, several of them were the subject of press releases.  My co-authors were amongst the biggest names in the field, and our work had important impact in the sub-fields they addressed.

At 28, I was the youngest member elected to the [Royal Society of Edinburgh’s Young Academy of Scotland](http://youngacademyofscotland.org.uk), an interdisciplinary grouping of academics, businesspeople, civil service and third sector workers, formed to act as a voice for the people of Scotland.

I had co-supervised a PhD student to a very successful thesis and future career, alongside many, many undergraduate projects.

I was a regular fixture on the BBC (I appeared on TV on a yearly basis, for programmes including Stargazing Live, The Sky at Night and Horizon).

I’d just finished [my first textbook](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Solving-Fermis-Paradox-Cambridge-Astrobiology/dp/110716365X) (with the last leg being very sleep-deprived, as we cared for our newborn daughter).

And I still couldn’t get a job.

I’ve been very fortunate to have excellent mentors, who have encouraged me to follow my own ideas rather than whatever is academically “fashionable”.  They appreciated my value, and found ways to keep me employed.  I spent years on six-month and twelve-month extensions, with the sword of Damocles always hanging over.

In spring 2018, I watched that scene again:

![0bf9b9b0d27f82edd6bdc51f32ed431e](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/0bf9b9b0d27f82edd6bdc51f32ed431e.jpg)

And I raged inwardly.  **Batman, you’re a fucking idiot**, I thought.  **Catwoman is right, you don’t owe these people anything!**

I was very surprised by my reaction.  After all, he’s saving the lives of millions by removing a nuclear device from Gotham’s city centre, and he lives at the end.  I still couldn’t avoid that visceral, irrational, gut reaction I had to someone giving everything and not being recognised.  Few would ever know it was Bruce Wayne that saved the city.

That gut reaction started to grow, and take root somewhere deep in me.  My work was well recognised - I was invited to give prestigious review talks, join grant panels and telescope allocation committees, but never to interview for fellowships.  Senior colleagues came to me asking for my expertise, but I couldn't parlay these encounters into tangible benefits for me.

I had chosen not to follow fashion, and I found myself at odds with the trends that selected people for advancement.  I had watched departmental politics manoeuvre me away from positions of responsibility that could help me advance, frustrated but ultimately helpless.  Who would listen to someone whose contract is measured in months?

![the-dark-knight-rises-tom-hardy-bane-image1.jpg](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/the-dark-knight-rises-tom-hardy-bane-image1.jpg)

Academia has a whole host of problems that profoundly affect its ability to acquire and generate knowledge - [toxic work culture](https://www.theguardian.com/higher-education-network/2018/may/18/academia-exploitation-university-mental-health-professors-plagiarism), [chronic under-diversity](https://physicstoday.scitation.org/doi/10.1063/PT.3.3536), [sexual harassment](https://www.geekwire.com/2016/astronomy-sexual-harassment-hashtag/), [burnout](https://www.apadivisions.org/division-28/publications/newsletters/psychopharmacology/2018/07/burnout-academia), dwindling funding and perverse metrics [encouraging researchers to pursue bad science](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/full/10.1098/rsos.160384).  For me, the worst part of it all is the small amounts of hope it feeds its itinerant, insecure, in-thrall workforce.

We all sit in “how to win a fellowship” seminars, which are very good, and filled with sensible advice and ideas, but can’t change the underlying truth about how to get a fellowship.  In the end, the winners are the **lucky** - publishing a popular paper/papers at the right point in the fellowship cycle, building the right social network early on as a PhD student, submitting applications at the right time when the applicant pool is biased in your favour.  Once your work reaches a certain quality, you join such a crowded field of talent that award committees [struggle to do better than random](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/481655v2) in picking “the best” candidates.

Despite consuming [article](https://www.theguardian.com/higher-education-network/2018/jan/26/we-need-a-bigger-conversation-about-bullying-in-academia) [after](https://academicladder.com/toxic-environments-7-steps-to-change) [depressing](http://www.nextscientist.com/leave-academia-before-postdocs/) [article](https://thesiswhisperer.com/2013/02/13/academic-assholes/) about how awful my chosen career was, I persisted.  I continued to eat the morsels of hope.  “Just keep going”, was the advice I received.  “The system is rubbish, but your luck will eventually come in.  Staying power wins the day.”

![DnJgDi5XgAAbTdy](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/dnjgdi5xgaabtdy.jpg)

So I stayed.  I started to see the fellowship cycle as a boxing match.  I kept my gloves up and took the punches, and kept swinging my fists, even as my own self-worth began to crumble and I fell into depression.

My pay never increased in real terms in eight years, and I never had a permanent contract.

Both mine and my wife’s commutes were starting to wear us out.  We had lived in our house for four years, and yet we didn’t hang up pictures, because in our hearts we knew we would probably have to leave at relatively short notice for my career, one way or the other.  We had a baby and we couldn’t plan for her future.  My PhD cohort had either left academia or got their fellowships years ago, and had moved onto lectureships and readerships.

I loved my work, but it returned my love with heartache.  For the unfortunate, academia is very much like an abusive relationship.  It hurts you again and again, but you stay out of love for it, hoping you can change it for the better.

![dark-knight-prison.jpg](/images/posts/on-leaving-academia/dark-knight-prison.jpg)

Watching Bruce Wayne ascend the climb to escape his prison, on that spring day in 2018, something clicked in me.  I was in a prison of my own making.  And in some ways, so were my mentors, the people that I respected most in my profession.  They were stressed, overworked, scraping for funding in a cash-starved environment, which will most likely be devastated by Brexit.  One of my dearest mentors is [considering leaving the country](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/people/interview-catherine-heymans).

I was luckier than Bruce Wayne.  I didn’t even have to climb out of my prison.  I just needed to be brave enough to open the door and walk out.  Once I realised this, things started to unsnarl inside me at last.

I took a temporary lectureship, to give me a chance to taste the last bit of academia I hadn’t really experienced - teaching.  It was a heavy teaching load, and my colleagues were surprised that I could keep hold of it and still [continue to publish](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05385).  But my motivation had changed.  I was going to jump - I wasn’t going to be pushed.  Hell, I was like a ski-jumper hurtling down a ramp, and at the end of the ramp I was leaping to freedom.

I’ve now landed from that jump.  Already, our family life is far better.  I'm starting a new career with a permanent job and a slight salary boost, and my commute will take half the time at about a quarter of the cost.  My wife’s commute is even shorter, relatively speaking.  Most importantly, we will finally be able to plan for our daughter’s future.

I still have mixed feelings about my journey.  I’m immensely proud of my accomplishments, and of those it will be my mentees I remember best, not the manuscripts.

I’m an astrophysicist, and I will be long after my name stops appearing in ApJ, MNRAS and IJA.  I wear this as part of my identity, and no one can take it from me.

(In all the noise about assessing impact and knowledge transfer, the one thing that academia never gets appropriate credit for is the talented people it injects into the world.  If only REF impact cases truly took account of how academics enrich the worlds they wander into - but that’s another article)

I’m also immensely sad to leave a profession I trained hard for, and for which I had lots more to give.  I’ve had quite a bit of time to think why I didn’t make it to a permanent academic job, and I’ll keep my own counsel on the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

It took the greatest effort to realise that if my talents weren’t wanted, I should take them somewhere that they are wanted.  That might seem trivial to people in most careers, but to your average postdoc, internalising that statement takes tremendous strength - or a straw to finally break the camel’s back.

In a way, I will be grieving for the academic life I lost.  I’m really lucky that in those darker moments, I will be able to look at my family, rooted at last, surrounded by the people we love and care about most.

I will know this: that life I lost - it's not the best life there is for me out there.

In the words of Thomas Wayne:

> “Why do we fall? So we can learn to pick ourselves up."
